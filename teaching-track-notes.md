# Teaching-Track Job Market Reflections

I went on the academic job market for (mostly) R1 teaching-track roles in Computer Science in 2025–2026, in the last year of my (long) PhD. I'm sharing a few reflections and notes on my job market experience here, but this page is not meant to be an authoritative guide to the teaching-track job market. A great place to start is the [CS Job Market Guide](https://csguides.github.io/grad-job-guide/), which is a general guide to post-PhD academic career job searches. Adam Blank and Travis McGaha maintain a [list of CS teaching-track jobs that do not require a PhD](https://adamblan.github.io/cs-ms-teaching-track/), which is another good resource for folks interested in teaching careers who do not have a PhD (note that a Master's is required for these roles). [Sylvia Herbert](https://sylviaherbert.com/faculty-application-advice) has a good set of resources based on her job search, as does my former Berkeley classmate [Ryan Cosner](https://www.rkcosner.com/blogs/faculty_apps/).

## Picking Schools

Making "your list" of schools to which you will apply is a big part of the search. If you have teaching-track faculty mentors, these are your _best resources_. For me, these folks were [Suraj Rampure](https://eecs.engin.umich.edu/people/rampure-suraj/) and [Andrew DeOrio](https://andrewdeorio.com/) at Michigan. The more these people interact with the larger CS teaching-track community, going to conferences like SIGCSE, the better. This is just my observation here, but it seems easier for teaching-track faculty to remain a bit insulated from the broader community: Michigan CS has multiple "lifers", who did all their schooling or at least their MS/PhDs at Michigan and stuck around. That makes it a little tough to get a sense of what the broader hiring market is like, particularly if faculty have been at their school for a while.

I applied nationwide, but many folks applying to teaching-track roles have personal reasons to aim for a region or two (family, partner's career, etc.). My sense of geographically constrained searches is that they are more viable for teaching-track careers than research/tenure-track roles, in which big R1s are really the only option for a certain kind of career. On the other hand, major regions may have multiple institutions that value teaching even if there's only one or two R1 schools: think R2, liberal arts schools, or other regional universities that mostly offer undergraduate degrees. For this example, this might look like applying to Stanford and Berkeley in the Bay Area, but also San Jose State, University of San Francisco, and San Francisco State University.
I had the greatest success at, and mostly applied to, R1 teaching-track jobs. I did interview at one PUI, Santa Clara University, but my background is at large public R1s and ultimately that kind of school felt like the best fit for me. If you're interested in other sorts of institutions, the [CS-PUI guide](https://cs-pui.github.io/) is a great resource. I can't comment on how well my advice below will apply to those types of schools.

## Instructor of Record experience and how to get it

Despite spending six years in my PhD, I did not have an opportunity to be instructor of record (IoR) at Michigan and had to teach elsewhere, at UC San Diego's [Halıcıoğlu Data Science Institute](https://datascience.ucsd.edu/), in Summer 2024. For those reading this, at least one class of IoR experience has been described to me as "necessary but not sufficient" experience to get a teaching-track job. Effectively, you need to have some experience teaching CS as the primary instructor and not just a TA in order to get a full-time teaching faculty role. This makes sense to me, but not many institutions let their graduate students serve as primary instructors. If you're either an undergraduate student interested in teaching careers or a grad student looking for IoR opportunities, please email me! I am happy to give you advice based on my understanding of the landscape but am not putting details on this page due to the ever-changing nature of department policies on who gets to teach what.

## CS vs other fields?

Ultimately, I ended up at NYU's Center for Data Science. I have a Robotics PhD, technically, but I did all my research in computer science and taught only one Robotics class. My most natural fit was in Computer Science roles, but my technical and teaching background helped me get offers for Data Science roles. Despite not holding any data science degrees, I've taught more Data Science than anything: Berkeley's Data 8 as an undergrad TA, Michigan's Practical Data Science as a head TA, and at UC San Diego's Halıcıoğlu Data Science Institute (HDSI) as an instructor of record. At NYU this was explicitly an offer from CDS, which was doing its own search. At Arizona and Vanderbilt, I received either joint offers or offers from the CS department with either written or verbal discussions of courses I'd teach related to Data Science. At Penn, my offer was joint with the Department of Mechanical Engineering and Applied Mechanics (MEAM) to teach robotics and control theory courses, again related to my technical background.

## MS vs PhD

I went on the market in the last year of my PhD, and thus could promise to have a PhD degree in hand by the time I started as faculty. Around half of my interviews would also have hired folks without PhDs (Master's degrees required). Again, the [No-PhD site](https://adamblan.github.io/cs-ms-teaching-track/) is a better resource than my recollection, but these were, off the top of my head, Arizona, Washington, Penn, and UNC. I _do not think having a PhD has made me a better teacher_ but it has **given me more time spent teaching**. That is to say, very little about doing graduate-level research feels like it has prepared me for a full-time teaching career, but I have had many more semesters to TA disparate classes and, critically, more summers in which I could have served as instructor of record. If you're going from undergrad to a one- or two-year Master's program, you have only one or two summers to get an IoR job. I had many more, if you count all the way back to my undergrad days and "gap year" working in industry. That alone is not enough of a reason to do a PhD if you're only interested in teaching; eventually you will be like Wile E. Coyote running off a cliff, looking down, and realizing your passion for teaching did not translate into enough motivation to earn a PhD. (I had this moment myself around summer 2023 and becoming a TA in Fall 2023 is the only reason I stuck around to finish my degree.) I saw an article that says you can only do a PhD for yourself, not for anyone else, and that rings true for me ([Ronald Azuma's "So Long, and Thanks for the Ph.D."](https://www.cs.unc.edu/~azuma/hitch4.html)). Your independent passion for research has to carry you through four to seven years of grad school, not a love for teaching, unfortunately. Again, email me if this is something you're struggling with.

If you have multiple choices of MS degrees in front of you, please reach out to teaching faculty at each of those schools. They are, almost universally, wonderfully welcoming people who are happy to chat with aspiring CS teachers. They'll have good advice about what the teaching landscape looks like for students at their institutions. If you're in an MS degree and feel like somehow you've missed the boat, do not despair. A PhD is a long, poorly compensated journey, and there are multiple ways to gain teaching experience while in industry. You will, to be sure, have to forge your own path in some ways, but teaching at, say, community colleges or part-time during summers on the side of an industry job is absolutely a thing and a pathway to teaching careers. One person who's very passionate about getting more folks into CS teaching is Washington's [Justin Hsia](https://www.cs.washington.edu/people/faculty/justin-hsia/), who has studied the needs of CS-teaching institutions, and may provide good guidance about community college teaching in particular.

## Interviewing

I had eight on-campus interviews stretching from mid-January to mid-March 2026. With SIGCSE in the middle, that meant I had a trip a week for eight out of nine weeks, minus a rescheduled interview due to severe weather and flight cancellations. You should plan for very little research progress during that time.

If you're a PhD student, get your advisor to fund you for _research_, and do not teach a class. My advisor had me on a GSRA (Michigan's version of research funding for a semester) and that meant I could focus basically entirely on interviews. I didn't have to schedule around lab sections or get office hours covered, which was a _boon_. My ideal interview schedule was to fly during afternoons (I'm not a morning person), ideally during weekdays. For example, if I interviewed on a Tuesday, I flew in Monday ~2pm or so, and departed Wednesday ~11am. This travel schedule let me get full nights of sleep before and after my interviews but still permitted alternative flights earlier or later in the day if there were cancellations. As interview season is during the winter, travel to the Northeast or Midwest regions might be impacted.

Travel tips:

- Do not schedule more than one interview per week, if you can avoid it. I found my interviews and travel quite tiring, even as a person who flies a lot for personal travel.
- Similarly, a Friday/Monday interview schedule can be a pain, even though you've technically split the two interviews across two weeks. This is particularly annoying because schools usually cover two nights of hotel, so you're left figuring out a Saturday night hotel yourself.
- Prioritize convenience in your travel: fly direct and take Uber/Lyft/taxi from the airport. Schools will reimburse you for reasonable travel expenses and spending (reimbursed) money to save time and hassle is well worth it.
- I wore the exact same outfit for every interview, plus or minus jackets and cardigans depending on weather. I wore a light blue button-down shirt, tucked into olive green chinos over brown dress shoes. See the CS Job Market Guide for more, but dress in a way that will make you comfortable; if you are not a frequent suit or blazer wearer, do not start doing so for the first time during your interviews.

## Opinionation

Anyone who knows me will tell you I do not hesitate before sharing what I think, or perhaps do not think before I speak. I was worried that this would be detrimental when I interviewed, but I think in fact my candor was a benefit. To be clear, you shouldn't be offensive (and neither was I!), but I think a candidate who has some strong opinions about teaching philosophy is not a bad thing. On a second visit, someone mentioned other candidates who had pedagogically excellent teaching demos (active learning, a blend of modalities, etc) but in a way that was "thin" —— perhaps they weren't able to express the beliefs behind their pedagogical choices.

Perhaps due to all the time I've spent in school as both a student and a TA, I have lots of opinions about things:

- If you read directly from your slides, you should be sent to jail. (Just kidding, but still, please never do this.)
- Math should be done by hand _always_, otherwise it's too easy to breeze through difficult derivation and computation. Hand-writing math when teaching is a natural check on your speed, and so I'll always use a whiteboard or iPad to present math.
- Tools matter, and should be optimized: things like Jupyter Notebooks are great for code-heavy lectures, etc. Talking about why you picked the platforms you did is a good thing.
- In-person office hours are just better, but I can understand why large courses choose virtual office hours to manage queues.

Maybe none of these are super controversial points, but I have arrived at them after many semesters of TA and instructor experience across lots of classes and three universities. Being able to back up my choices and explain classes that did things well or had room for improvement was, I think, a strength when I was interviewing.

## Job Talks

Like with tenure-track roles, schools will ask you to give a job talk. In this case, the job talk is a sample lecture. For teaching-track roles, your audience will be both faculty and students. Almost always, the faculty will be acting as students, asking questions to which they surely know the answer, to assess how you respond. One thing I did was a brief "meta" slide at the beginning of my demos laying out things like the class I'd aim a lecture at, or what I'd expect of my students in terms of knowledge going into the lecture. After that, I said something like:

> I'm going to switch into teacher-student mode now, so if you'd like me to break the fourth wall, please say so explicitly in your question.

Then I kicked off the sample lecture as if it were a real class. I found teaching demos to be good exercises but a little contrived compared to real-life teaching. This is because of a few things:

- You want to tell a "full story", which can be hard in just 30 or 60 minutes. Be careful selecting topics and make sure you can motivate and introduce a problem, and then fully present a solution (an algorithm or something else). Compared to a lecture you might give in a typical class, you'll have to spend more time introducing and concluding than just picking up from, say, last Thursday's class.
- You want to cover lots of aspects of good teaching. You don't need to pack **everything** in: don't burden your demo with active learning, live coding, hand-writing math, group exercises, proofs, videos, etc. But you do want to show the faculty and students evaluating you that you can do more than one thing, and have a general knowledge of what good teaching looks like. That meant I did try to blend in a few of the above: some active learning in every demo, for sure (think-pair-share is sufficient in my experience), and made sure to deliberately live code or hand-write math when relevant. In a real, say, Machine Learning class, you might have one very proof-heavy and not-very-interactive lecture when you do things like derive bias-variance, but that, to me, would be a bad demo for showing off all your skills as a teacher.

One annoying thing about teaching-track interviews is that a different job talk might be required for ~every school at which you interview. Times vary widely, from 30-60 minutes usually, as do target courses. Some schools give you a topic (recursion for me in one case), while others share a list of topics, and still others leave things entirely open-ended. Based on the teaching need, you might be expected to cover introductory material or aim all the way for graduate-level coursework. This meant I had to prepare 4 different talks, and add/subtract material to make sure the timing was right. At one school, I was asked to teach an actual 75-minute lecture section. This was, by far, the most fun I had giving a teaching demo, but I am still shocked (and grateful!) that another instructor trusted me (a random guy, more or less) with their linear regression lecture. In general, be prepared to put together multiple teaching demos for your various job talks. This means you might also have to schedule multiple practice talks on short notice. I do recommend doing practice talks for the first (or maybe even second) time you run a talk, but at some point I stopped practicing talks that I had largely given before. Again, this is a _great_ place for your teaching faculty mentors to help you out and give feedback. If you're a PhD student, your advisor and labmates may not know as much about what teaching demos look like.

I did not interview at Berkeley, but have heard they ask for a vision talk in addition to a concrete teaching demo.

## Student Meetings

I had a ton of fun meeting with students, and was a big fan of institutions that scheduled those for me. At several schools, students also took me out to lunch. Shout out to the University of Texas at Austin, where I really enjoyed my interview and my _two_ student meetings. Please take these student meetings seriously; students are often asked to evaluate teaching-track candidates just like faculty are. Pretty much all of the students I met with were TAs, so I took that time as an opportunity to speak to folks who might literally be on my staff a few months later, if I were lucky enough to get and accept a job offer. I often asked questions about the teaching-track experience, staff training opportunities, how office hours run, what TAs do, whether staff move between classes or stick with the same course, etc.
